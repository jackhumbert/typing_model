Thumbline

Your Colemak Ortho board, taken apart with a thumb-movement model and rebuilt for how phones are actually typed on — one thumb or two, precision first, no autocorrect to catch a miss.

What the model says about your Colemak

The desktop model in this repo scores keymaps by walking a corpus through per-key efforts and stroke-path penalties. This adaptation swaps the machinery for thumb physics: same-side taps follow the thumb Fitts curves measured for the KALQ study (CHI 2013), alternating taps use its hover-over model — the idle thumb drifts toward its next target while the other presses — and precision is a 2 mm tap-scatter Gaussian against each key's exclusive cell. Hover any key below to see its share of taps and average press cost.

Colemak is already a very good two-thumb phone layout — and a local optimum. Its left-consonants / right-vowels split alternates sides on 52% of transitions — exactly the property the KALQ optimizer chases — and a validated hill-climb over all 325 possible letter swaps found none that improves the balanced objective by even 0.4%: there is no cheap tweak worth relearning two keys for. The value sits in bigger moves. Full re-annealing lifts alternation to ~60% and pulls the vowels back within one-thumb reach of the common consonants; and the 10-column grid itself leaves precision on the table — a geometry problem, not a letter problem.

The three levers

Alternation two thumbs

When the wait is short, an alternating tap is cheaper than a same-side one — the idle thumb is already hovering over its target. Colemak's vowel split banks this; any redesign has to defend it.

Key size precision

Serial thumb taps want ≥ 7.6–7.7 mm targets (Parhi et al. 2006). On a 65 mm screen, ten columns miss that bar; eight clear it.

Travel one thumb

One thumb has no partner to alternate with — only distance matters. It wants the frequent letters in one compact cluster above space, which is precisely what a split layout refuses to do. This is where optimization pays.

The candidates

Every variant keeps your bottom row (123 · ⌫ · #+= · space · return), your punctuation zone, and the numbers/symbols layers of the original board. The 8-column family moves shift down beside 123 and hides the four rarest letters (q z x j — 0.45% of characters) behind long-presses on mnemonic hosts: q→u  z→s  x→c  j→g, shown in key corners.

The swipe family

A different bargain, from the 3×3 tap-plus-swipe grids of Android (MessagEase and its open-source successor Thumb-Key): nine enormous keys — a third of the screen wide each — carry the nine most frequent letters as plain taps (~70% of characters), and everything else is a short directional swipe. Tap misses become physically implausible; the residual risks are the swipe itself (slower than a tap, and diagonals fumble more — Yamanaka et al., CHI 2024) and direction slips, both charged in the numbers below.

xKeyboard can't express gestures, so these can't ship as .xkeyboard files — but classic MessagEase is alive on the App Store and hosts exactly this grid on an iPhone today. The corpus-tuned variant below is what the model would build from your statistics under Thumb-Key's construction rules — and it beats the classic by barely 1%, which is the interesting result: the family's structure does all the work. If you go this way, learn the standard MessagEase layout; it's already at the ceiling and works in the shipping app.

All the numbers

WPM figures are expert-ceiling estimates from movement models fitted in a tablet study; read them comparatively, not as a forecast of day-one speed. Error risk = Gaussian tap-miss at 2 mm scatter, plus assumed direction-slip rates (1% cardinal / 3% diagonal) for swipe layouts; it scales with your true σ — the ranking doesn't change. Swipe/hold % is the share of characters needing a gesture beyond a plain tap. Corpus: Alice in Wonderland + this repo's corpus passage + mobile-register phrases upweighted ×20 (~180k characters).

Trying them on the phone

  1. Grab the .xkeyboard files from ios/layouts/ on the branch — AirDrop, Files, or iCloud them to the phone.
  2. Open each with the XKeyboard app (share sheet → XKeyboard) to import; every variant carries a fresh keyboard ID, so they install side by side instead of overwriting your Colemak.
  3. Give a candidate a fair run before judging: the KALQ study needed a few hours of practice before its optimized layout overtook QWERTY, then finished 34% faster. Expect the same shape of curve, gentler here because your function keys and punctuation don't move.
  4. To iterate further: edit the corpus in ios/corpus/ toward your real writing, re-run python3 ios/explore.py, and the annealer + exact polish will re-derive the boards from your own statistics.

Model: KALQ same-side / alternating thumb equations (Oulasvirta et al., CHI 2013) · Fitts-digraph corpus scoring (MacKenzie & Zhang 1999; Zhai, Hunter & Smith 2000) · target-size threshold (Parhi, Karlson & Bederson 2006) · tap-scatter precision (Azenkot & Zhai 2012; Bi, Li & Zhai 2013).
